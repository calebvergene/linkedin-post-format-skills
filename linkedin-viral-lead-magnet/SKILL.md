# LinkedIn Viral Lead Magnet Post

Write a post that gets as much reach as possible by teasing a comprehensive analysis (8–16 hours of work) of well-known names.

## Inputs

| Input | | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | Required | If it's missing, ask. |
| Result / metric | Optional | e.g. "100k views", "500 leads", "$40k MRR". |
| Timeframe | Optional | e.g. "60 days". |
| Target audience | Optional | Sets the vocabulary, the pain points and which examples to use. |
| Context from the author | Optional | Stories, facts, numbers, the resource on offer, tone preferences. This is the most valuable input. |

- **Use the author's context as written and never contradict it.** If they say it took 90 days, don't write 60.
- **Never invent proof.** Don't make up metrics, client names, testimonials, follower counts or "used by 500+" claims. If the post needs a number the user didn't give, write a bracketed placeholder such as `[X]` or `[# of people who've used it]` and list the placeholders after the post.
- **There has to be a real resource.** If the user hasn't said what will be sent, ask, or write `[RESOURCE]` and point it out. A comment CTA with nothing behind it costs the author trust.
- Don't stop to ask about optional fields. Draft with what you have and note what would make the post stronger.

## Structure

1. **Hook**: an impressive case study or analysis, e.g. "I analyzed 100 of Hormozi's top posts. 9 patterns showed up again and again."
2. **Tease the effort**: "I spent [8+] hours breaking down…" The work invested is what makes it valuable.
3. **What's inside**: 6–10 `→` bullets listing frameworks, strategies and examples ("→ The 3-part hook formula behind his 5 most-shared posts").
4. **Name-drop recognizable figures or brands** in the hook and the bullets. Familiar names stop people scrolling.
5. **Triple CTA**:
   "Want it?
   1. Like this post
   2. Comment 'BREAKDOWN'
   3. Connect with me (so I can DM you)"
6. **Bonus**: "I'll also send you a 30-min walkthrough video." Only include it if one exists or will exist; otherwise use a placeholder.

Advice:
- Only name-drop people or brands whose public work is actually analyzed. Don't imply they endorse the author or worked with them.
- If the analysis doesn't exist yet, write the post with placeholders and tell the author what they need to produce before posting.
- If the user gave a result or timeframe, use them as credibility, e.g. "These same frameworks got me 100k views in 60 days."

**Length:** 1,300–2,200 characters (LinkedIn's limit is 3,000).

## Formatting rules

**1. Hook: open with a bold, attention-grabbing first line.**
LinkedIn hides everything after roughly the first 2–3 lines (about 200 characters on desktop, less on mobile) behind "…see more". Lines 1–2 must earn the click. "Bold" means bold content, not bold formatting. Patterns that work:
- A specific result: "We went from 0 to 12,000 followers in 90 days."
- A contrarian claim: "Cold email isn't dead. Your first line is."
- Time or effort invested: "I spent 11 hours breaking down 40 of Apple's launch emails."
- A curiosity gap with a number: "7 hiring mistakes that cost me $200k."
- A direct callout to the audience: "Founders: stop writing your own LinkedIn posts like blog intros."
- Personal credibility with recognizable names: "I was able to land big tech interviews at Netflix, Google, and TikTok by following a straightforward roadmap." This works because it's true and it's the author's own result, not a claim about anyone else.

Avoid warm-ups such as "I'm excited to share", "In today's world" and "Let's talk about…", and avoid starting with a question the reader can answer "no" to.

The second line should either add tension ("Most people get this wrong."), promise a payoff ("Here's exactly how:"), or reduce the topic to a few parts ("It comes down to two things:" followed by a short numbered list).

**2. Short sentences, generous line breaks.**
Keep sentences to about 1–2 lines on mobile. Put a blank line between most lines. Most paragraphs should be 1–2 sentences. A 3–5 sentence paragraph is fine once or twice, where the author is talking personally (answering an objection, "If I were to go back…"). Mixing very short lines with the odd longer paragraph reads more human than one-liners all the way down. White space is what makes a post skimmable on LinkedIn.

**3. Visual hierarchy using plain-text symbols.**
- `→` for benefits, results and "this leads to that" lines
- `-` for plain lists
- `1.` `2.` `3.` for steps or ranked points

**Section headers in Unicode bold.** When a post has 2–3 distinct sections, give each one a header line in Unicode sans-serif bold, e.g. `𝗚𝗲𝘁𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘃𝗶𝗲𝘄: 𝗬𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘂𝗺𝗲`, with two blank lines before it. These are ordinary Unicode characters (Mathematical Sans-Serif Bold: A–Z start at U+1D5D4, a–z at U+1D5EE, 0–9 at U+1D7EC), not markdown, so LinkedIn displays them as bold. Only use them for headers, never for whole sentences: screen readers and LinkedIn search don't read them as normal text.

`→` and `-` are optional. A post organized by bold section headers and numbered lists doesn't need arrows.

Use a few emoji at most, and only if the author's voice uses them. Don't put an emoji at the start of every line.

**4. Specifics everywhere.**
Write exact numbers, timeframes and results ("37%", "in 21 days", "from 3 to 41 demos/month"), not "a lot", "quickly" or "significant growth". Specific details are what make a post believable. (They still have to come from the author; see Inputs.)

**5. Social proof or credibility markers.**
Examples: who it was used by, results it produced, the author's relevant experience ("After running ads for 40+ SaaS brands…"), or well-known names the work analyzes. Only use proof the author supplied, or placeholders.

**6. A clear comment CTA: "Comment '[WORD]' and I'll send it to you."**
- Choose one short, all-caps keyword that matches the resource: GUIDE, TEMPLATE, PLAYBOOK, SWIPE, BREAKDOWN, CHECKLIST.
- Keep it to one word that's easy to type on a phone and not something people would comment anyway (avoid YES or WOW).
- Comments push the post's distribution, and the keyword shows the author who wants the resource.
- An acceptable alternative: "drop your email in the comments and I'll share the doc with you!" This works well for a Google Doc or Notion guide, and the author can share it straight to those emails.

**7. Scannable.**
A reader who only sees the first two lines plus anything formatted with `→`, `-` or numbers should still get the main point.

**Tone:** conversational and direct, written in the first person. Use "you" more than "I" except in story parts. Cut filler, every line should earn its place. The post must be useful even to someone who never comments.

**Hashtags:** none by default. If the user wants them, add 0–3 relevant ones at the very end.

## Output

- **Output only the post text, ready to paste into LinkedIn.** Plain text, no markdown (no `**bold**`, no `#` headings), and no preamble like "Here's your post:". Unicode such as `→` and Unicode-bold section headers is fine.
- After the post, separated by a line, add a short note **only if needed**: placeholders to fill in, inputs that would make it stronger, or an assumption you made.
- If the user asks for options, give 2–3 alternative hooks (just lines 1–2) rather than whole new posts. The hook is what gets tested.

## Quality check before returning

- Would lines 1–2 make the target reader click "see more"?
- Is every number either from the author or a visible `[placeholder]`?
- Can someone get the main point by skimming only the hook, the section headers and the `→`, `-` or numbered lines?
- Does it end with the triple CTA (like, comment keyword, connect), plus the bonus only if one exists?
- If there's a comment keyword, does it match the resource, and is it one word in all caps?
- Is it under 3,000 characters, ideally 1,300–2,200 characters?
- Does it contain no markdown, no throat-clearing opener and no generic filler ("in today's fast-paced world", "game-changer")?
- Is it useful to a reader who never comments?

## Example

Illustrative numbers. In a real post every number comes from the author or stays a `[placeholder]`.

Inputs: topic = SaaS pricing pages; result = 100k views; timeframe = 60 days; context = analyzed pricing pages of 30 well-known SaaS companies, recorded a walkthrough.

```
I broke down the pricing pages of Notion, Figma, Linear, Slack and 26 other SaaS giants.

It took me 11 hours.

Here's what the best ones all do (and most startups miss):

→ The "decoy tier" Slack uses to push you to Pro
→ Why Linear shows only 3 plans (and hides the 4th)
→ Figma's annual-billing toggle trick
→ The 1-line FAQ that cuts Notion's support tickets
→ 5 layouts ranked by how clear they are
→ 8 frameworks you can copy this week

These same frameworks helped me get 100k views in 60 days writing about pricing.

I put it all into one breakdown.

Want it?

1. Like this post
2. Comment "BREAKDOWN"
3. Connect with me (so I can DM you)

I'll also send you a 30-min walkthrough video.
```
