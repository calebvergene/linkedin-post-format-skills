# LinkedIn Resource Post

Write a post that gives away the author's whole roadmap for free, then offers the full guide (a doc, Notion page, or PDF) to anyone who comments. The post itself teaches the method; the resource adds depth, templates and examples. A post that's genuinely useful on its own earns more trust, saves and shares than a teaser.

## Inputs

| Input | | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | Required | If it's missing, ask. |
| Result | Optional | The author's own outcome, ideally with recognizable names: e.g. "interviews at Netflix, Google, TikTok". |
| Timeframe | Optional | e.g. "60 days". |
| Target audience | Required | Who the post is for, e.g. "high school juniors applying to US colleges". If it's missing, ask. Don't guess. It sets the CTA qualifier ("If you're…"), the vocabulary and which examples to use. |
| Resource | Required | What the author will send: e.g. "10-page Google Doc". If it's missing, ask or use `[RESOURCE]`. |
| Context from the author | Optional | The steps they actually followed, numbers, tools, stories, tone preferences. This is the most valuable input; the roadmap comes from here, not from generic advice. |

- **Use the author's context as written and never contradict it.** If they say it took 90 days, don't write 60.
- **Numbers can be filled in, names can't.** If the author didn't give a number (practice tests, drafts, years, problems solved, attempts), use a realistic one so the post reads complete, and list the numbers you chose in the note after the post so the author can swap in their own.
- **Anything you'd tag or name stays a placeholder** unless the author gave it: schools, companies, employers, people, programs. Use `[COLLEGE]`, `[COMPANY]`, `[COMPANY 1], [COMPANY 2], and [COMPANY 3]` and so on. Never invent client names or testimonials. Public tools and resources the audience already knows (Neetcode 150, Bluebook, the Common App) are fine to name. The placeholder rule is for names that are part of the author's own story.
- **There has to be a real resource.** If the user hasn't said what will be sent, ask, or write `[RESOURCE]` and point it out. A comment CTA with nothing behind it costs the author trust.
- Don't stop to ask about optional fields. Draft with what you have and note what would make the post stronger.

## Structure

Modeled on a post by the skill's author that performed very well (the example below).

1. **Credibility hook**: the author's result plus recognizable names, then how simple the path was: "I was able to land big tech interviews at Netflix, Google, and TikTok by following a straightforward roadmap."
2. **Break it into 2–3 parts**: "It comes down to two things:" then a numbered list, one line per part, each tied to an outcome ("A strong resume to get the interview").
3. **One section per part**, each opened by a Unicode-bold header with two blank lines before it ("𝗣𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘃𝗶𝗲𝘄: 𝗟𝗲𝗲𝘁𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗲"). Inside each section:
   - **Concrete counts and rules**: "Get 3 projects", "Around 120 is enough", "stuck for more than 30 minutes".
   - **Named resources the audience already knows** (Neetcode 150, a specific course or tool) instead of generic advice.
   - **One myth-bust**: "You don't need to grind 500 problems." Name the scary number people assume, then the smaller real one.
   - **The step that makes you stand out**: "Your third project is where you stand out." Short punchy follow-ups such as "Something people actually use. Something that makes money."
4. **An objection in quotes, then the answer**: "“But nobody will hire me yet.”" Say the doubt the reader is thinking, then answer it honestly, including the unglamorous part ("It's not ideal, but…").
5. **A hindsight line**: "If I were to go back, this is the best way to start." This is where the author's voice comes through.
6. **"That's all there is to it."** then **a recap line made of the numbers**: "3 projects. 1-2 experiences. ~120 Leetcode problems." That line is what people screenshot and remember.
7. **Encouraging close**: "It's not complicated, but it does take consistency, and there's no better time to start than now."
8. **One CTA aimed at the audience**: "If you're [audience] and want the full [10-page guide] I followed, drop your email in the comments and I'll share the doc with you!" One CTA only, with no triple CTA or bonus. The audience qualifier ("If you're recruiting for Software Engineering") makes the right readers feel it's for them.

Advice:
- The post has to work on its own. Someone who never comments should still be able to follow the roadmap. The guide adds depth, templates and examples.
- The names in the hook must be the author's real result. Use exactly the companies and outcomes the author gave, or placeholders.
- Keep the tone humble and practical ("straightforward", "not complicated") rather than boastful. The result already does the bragging.
- Don't use `→` bullets just to use them. Short lines, numbered lists and the bold headers are enough structure.

**Length:** 1,400–2,200 characters (LinkedIn's limit is 3,000).

## Formatting rules

**1. Hook: open with a bold, attention-grabbing first line.**
LinkedIn hides everything after roughly the first 2–3 lines (about 200 characters on desktop, less on mobile) behind "…see more". Lines 1–2 must earn the click. "Bold" means bold content, not bold formatting. Patterns that work:
- A specific result: "We went from 0 to 12,000 followers in 90 days."
- A contrarian claim: "Cold email isn't dead. Your first line is."
- Time or effort invested: "I spent 11 hours breaking down 40 of Apple's launch emails."
- A curiosity gap with a number: "7 hiring mistakes that cost me $200k."
- A direct callout to the audience: "Founders: stop writing your own LinkedIn posts like blog intros."
- Personal credibility with recognizable names: "I was able to land big tech interviews at Netflix, Google, and TikTok by following a straightforward roadmap." This works because it's true and it's the author's own result, not a claim about anyone else.

Avoid warm-ups such as "I'm excited to share", "In today's world" and "Let's talk about…", and avoid starting with a question the reader can answer "no" to.

The second line should either add tension ("Most people get this wrong."), promise a payoff ("Here's exactly how:"), or reduce the topic to a few parts ("It comes down to two things:" followed by a short numbered list).

**2. Short sentences, generous line breaks.**
Keep sentences to about 1–2 lines on mobile. Put a blank line between most lines. Most paragraphs should be 1–2 sentences. A 3–5 sentence paragraph is fine once or twice, where the author is talking personally (answering an objection, "If I were to go back…"). Mixing very short lines with the odd longer paragraph reads more human than one-liners all the way down. White space is what makes a post skimmable on LinkedIn.

**3. Visual hierarchy using plain-text symbols.**
- `→` for benefits, results and "this leads to that" lines
- `-` for plain lists
- `1.` `2.` `3.` for steps or ranked points

**Section headers in Unicode bold.** When a post has 2–3 distinct sections, give each one a header line in Unicode sans-serif bold, e.g. `𝗚𝗲𝘁𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘃𝗶𝗲𝘄: 𝗬𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘂𝗺𝗲`, with two blank lines before it. These are ordinary Unicode characters (Mathematical Sans-Serif Bold: A–Z start at U+1D5D4, a–z at U+1D5EE, 0–9 at U+1D7EC), not markdown, so LinkedIn displays them as bold. Only use them for headers, never for whole sentences: screen readers and LinkedIn search don't read them as normal text.

`→` and `-` are optional. A post organized by bold section headers and numbered lists doesn't need arrows.

Use a few emoji at most, and only if the author's voice uses them. Don't put an emoji at the start of every line.

**4. Specifics everywhere.**
Write exact numbers, timeframes and results ("37%", "in 21 days", "from 3 to 41 demos/month"), not "a lot", "quickly" or "significant growth". Specific details are what make a post believable. (See Inputs for which ones you can fill in.)

**5. Social proof or credibility markers.**
Examples: who it was used by, results it produced, the author's relevant experience ("After running ads for 40+ SaaS brands…"), or well-known names the work analyzes. Numbers can be filled in, but names of clients, companies or people come from the author or stay placeholders.

**6. A clear comment CTA: "Comment '[WORD]' and I'll send it to you."**
- Choose one short, all-caps keyword that matches the resource: GUIDE, TEMPLATE, PLAYBOOK, SWIPE, BREAKDOWN, CHECKLIST.
- Keep it to one word that's easy to type on a phone and not something people would comment anyway (avoid YES or WOW).
- Comments push the post's distribution, and the keyword shows the author who wants the resource.
- An acceptable alternative: "drop your email in the comments and I'll share the doc with you!" This works well for a Google Doc or Notion guide, and the author can share it straight to those emails.

**7. Scannable.**
A reader who only sees the first two lines plus anything formatted with `→`, `-` or numbers should still get the main point.

**Tone:** conversational and direct, written in the first person. Use "you" more than "I" except in story parts. Cut filler, every line should earn its place. The post must be useful even to someone who never comments.

**Hashtags:** none by default. If the user wants them, add 0–3 relevant ones at the very end.

## Output

- **Output only the post text, ready to paste into LinkedIn.** Plain text, no markdown (no `**bold**`, no `#` headings), and no preamble like "Here's your post:". Unicode such as `→` and Unicode-bold section headers is fine.
- After the post, separated by a line, add a short note **only if needed**: placeholders to fill in, inputs that would make it stronger, or an assumption you made.
- If the user asks for options, give 2–3 alternative hooks (just lines 1–2) rather than whole new posts. The hook is what gets tested.

## Quality check before returning

- Would lines 1–2 make the target reader click "see more"?
- Is every school, company or person either from the author or a `[placeholder]`, and are the numbers you filled in listed in the note?
- Can someone get the main point by skimming only the hook, the section headers and the `→`, `-` or numbered lines?
- Does it end with one CTA aimed at the audience ("drop your email" or a comment keyword) for the full guide?
- If there's a comment keyword, does it match the resource, and is it one word in all caps?
- Is it under 3,000 characters, ideally 1,400–2,200 characters?
- Does it contain no markdown, no throat-clearing opener and no generic filler ("in today's fast-paced world", "game-changer")?
- Is it useful to a reader who never comments?

## Example

A real post by the skill's author that performed very well (1,719 characters). Match its structure and rhythm, not its topic.

Inputs: topic = how to land SWE interviews at big tech; result = interviews at Netflix, Google, TikTok; audience = students recruiting for software engineering; context = has a 10-page guide in a Google Doc.

```
I was able to land big tech interviews at Netflix, Google, and TikTok by following a straightforward roadmap.

It comes down to two things:
1. A strong resume to get the interview
2. Leetcode / interview skills to pass the interview


𝗚𝗲𝘁𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘃𝗶𝗲𝘄: 𝗬𝗼𝘂𝗿 𝗿𝗲𝘀𝘂𝗺𝗲

Get 3 projects onto your resume and GitHub.

For the first two, pick an online course that builds something you actually find cool, using a stack you want to learn (Python, React, whatever gets you excited).

Follow along, build it, ship it.

Your third project is where you stand out.

Build something at scale. Something people actually use. Something that makes money. That one project on its own can get your resume through screening.

Then add 1-2 SWE experiences on top.

“But nobody will hire me yet.”

Start cold emailing. Reach out to startups and offer to work for free. Ask about unpaid research at your university. It’s not ideal, but these are resume lines recruiters genuinely respect. If I were to go back, this is the best way to start.


𝗣𝗮𝘀𝘀𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘃𝗶𝗲𝘄: 𝗟𝗲𝗲𝘁𝗰𝗼𝗱𝗲

You don’t need to grind 500 problems.

Around 120 is enough, covering everything up through graphs.

Start with the Neetcode 150. If a problem has you stuck for more than 30 minutes, watch the video walkthrough. Focus on the pattern, not on memorizing the solution.

Log each problem you do while summarizing the solution.

That’s all there is to it.

3 projects. 1-2 experiences. ~120 Leetcode problems.

It’s not complicated, but it does take consistency, and there’s no better time to start than now.


If you’re recruiting for Software Engineering and want the full 10-page guide I followed, drop your email in the comments and I’ll share the doc with you!
```
