# LinkedIn Hot Take Post

Write a post that makes one strong claim and argues for it well enough that the reader either agrees hard or wants to reply. The comments push the post's reach, and the argument sets up whatever the author offers at the end.

## Inputs

| Input | | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Topic or take | Required | The opinion, or the topic to form one about. If it's missing, ask. |
| Target audience | Required | Who the post is for, e.g. "CS students trying to land their first internship". If it's missing, ask. Don't guess. It sets the vocabulary, the pain points and which examples to use. |
| Offer | Optional | What commenters get: a guide, a waitlist, a community, a template. Without one, end with a question instead of a keyword. |
| Context from the author | Optional | Their experience with the take, stories, numbers, tone preferences. This is the most valuable input. |

- **Use the author's context as written and never contradict it.** If they say it took 90 days, don't write 60.
- **Numbers can be filled in, names can't.** If the author didn't give a number (results, timeframes, counts, steps), use a realistic one so the post reads complete, and list the numbers you chose in the note after the post so the author can swap in their own.
- **Anything you'd tag or name stays a placeholder** unless the author gave it: clients, companies, employers, schools, people. Use `[CLIENT]`, `[COMPANY]` and so on. Never invent testimonials. Public tools, companies and resources the audience already knows are fine to name as general examples (e.g. "big tech"). The placeholder rule is for names that are part of the author's own story.
- **The take has to be the author's.** Sharpen their opinion; don't invent a more extreme one for engagement. If you had to pick the angle yourself, say so in the note.
- **A keyword CTA needs something real behind it.** If the author mentions an offer but not what it is, write `[OFFER]` and point it out.
- Don't stop to ask about optional fields. Draft with what you have and note what would make the post stronger.

## Structure

This format has a few core pieces and a set of optional ones. It is not a template. Don't fill in the same slots in the same order every time, or every post will read like the last one with the words swapped. Write every line fresh for the topic, the audience and the author's voice.

**Core (every post):**
1. **The take, in the first line**: one specific claim the audience will either agree with hard or want to argue with. State it as what *is* true or what to do, rather than as a denial of something else.
2. **The reasoning**: why it's true, as a chain the reader can follow (do this → which gets you that → which leads to the result). Usually under 1–2 Unicode-bold section headers.
3. **Specifics that make it believable**: what's actually expected, what you learn, what changes, with concrete details and numbers.
4. **The ending**: a comment-keyword CTA for something real (guide, waitlist, community), or a question that invites people to weigh in if there's nothing to offer.

**Optional (pick 1–3 that fit, and vary which ones you use):**
- How low the barrier is: the short list of what's actually required
- The common path and this path side by side
- The payoff: how the reader's resume, network or options look afterward
- The obvious pushback, answered
- The author's own experience backing the take
- Who the take applies to most
- A one-line close that restates the take in new words

Advice:
- Make the take specific enough to act on. "Networking matters" is a vibe; "10 coffee chats will do more for your job search than 100 cold applications" is a take.
- Argue for the path, without insulting the people on the other path.
- One take per post. A second claim splits the comments.

**Keep it varied.** Change where the optional pieces go, how long the paragraphs run, how the hook is built and how the post ends. Let the topic decide the shape.

**Length:** 900–1,800 characters (LinkedIn's limit is 3,000).

## Formatting rules

**1. Hook: open with a bold, attention-grabbing first line.**
LinkedIn hides everything after roughly the first 2–3 lines (about 200 characters on desktop, less on mobile) behind "…see more". Lines 1–2 must earn the click. "Bold" means bold content, not bold formatting. Kinds of hooks that work (write your own line for each; don't reuse stock phrasing):
- A specific result with a number and a timeframe
- A contrarian claim that flips something the audience believes
- The time or effort invested in what follows
- A curiosity gap built on a number (how many mistakes, how much they cost)
- A direct callout to the audience naming something they're doing wrong
- Personal credibility with recognizable names: the author's own result at well-known companies, schools or clients. This only works when it's true.

Avoid warm-ups such as "I'm excited to share", "In today's world" and "Let's talk about…", and avoid starting with a question the reader can answer "no" to.

The second line should add tension, promise a payoff, or reduce the topic to a few parts.

**2. Short sentences, generous line breaks.**
Keep sentences to about 1–2 lines on mobile. Split ideas into separate short paragraphs instead of clumping them: a numbered point's title goes on its own line with a blank line before its explanation, and a formula, an example or a takeaway each get their own paragraph. Put a blank line between most lines. Most paragraphs should be 1–2 sentences. A 3–5 sentence paragraph is fine once or twice, where the author is talking personally (answering an objection, or a personal aside). Mixing very short lines with the odd longer paragraph reads more human than one-liners all the way down. White space is what makes a post skimmable on LinkedIn.

Leave **two blank lines** between the opening (the hook plus the line or two that set up the post, roughly what shows above "…see more") and the body, so the intro reads as its own block.

**3. Visual hierarchy using plain-text symbols.**
- `→` for benefits, results and "this leads to that" lines
- `-` for plain lists
- `1.` `2.` `3.` for steps or ranked points

**Section headers in Unicode bold.** When a post has 2–3 distinct sections, give each one a header line in Unicode sans-serif bold, e.g. `𝗦𝘁𝗲𝗽 𝟭: 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗳𝗼𝘂𝗻𝗱𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻`, with two blank lines before it. These are ordinary Unicode characters (Mathematical Sans-Serif Bold: A–Z start at U+1D5D4, a–z at U+1D5EE, 0–9 at U+1D7EC), not markdown, so LinkedIn displays them as bold. Only use them for headers, never for whole sentences: screen readers and LinkedIn search don't read them as normal text.

`→` and `-` are optional. A post organized by bold section headers and numbered lists doesn't need arrows.

Use a few emoji at most, and only if the author's voice uses them. Don't put an emoji at the start of every line.

**4. Specifics everywhere.**
Write exact numbers, timeframes and results, not "a lot", "quickly" or "significant growth". Specific details are what make a post believable. (See Inputs for which ones you can fill in.)

**5. Social proof or credibility markers.**
Examples: who it was used by, results it produced, the author's relevant experience, or well-known names the work analyzes. Numbers can be filled in, but names of clients, companies or people come from the author or stay placeholders.

**6. A clear comment CTA: readers comment one keyword to get the resource.**
- Choose one short, all-caps keyword that matches the resource: GUIDE, TEMPLATE, PLAYBOOK, SWIPE, BREAKDOWN, CHECKLIST.
- Keep it to one word that's easy to type on a phone and not something people would comment anyway (avoid YES or WOW).
- Comments push the post's distribution, and the keyword shows the author who wants the resource.
- An acceptable alternative: ask people to drop their email in the comments. This works well for a Google Doc or Notion guide, and the author can share it straight to those emails.

**7. Scannable.**
A reader who only sees the first two lines plus anything formatted with `→`, `-` or numbers should still get the main point.

**Tone:** conversational and direct, written in the first person. Use "you" more than "I" except in story parts. Cut filler, every line should earn its place. The post must be useful even to someone who never comments.

**Write in the positive.** Say what to do, plainly. Negation-heavy lines read as AI-generated, so avoid:
- Stacks of "No…" fragments ("No fluff. No fancy design.")
- "Not X, but Y" or "It's not X. It's Y." contrast lines
- "Never…" / "Stop doing…" / "Don't…" commands, and "Just" right after a list of negatives
If something should be avoided, phrase it as the better alternative ("Lead each bullet with the result" rather than "Never start with 'Responsible for'"). A myth-bust or a "what to skip" section is fine, but write it as a plain statement, not a string of negatives.

**Hashtags:** none by default. If the user wants them, add 0–3 relevant ones at the very end.

## Output

- **Output only the post text, ready to paste into LinkedIn.** Plain text, no markdown (no `**bold**`, no `#` headings), and no preamble like "Here's your post:". Unicode such as `→` and Unicode-bold section headers is fine.
- After the post, separated by a line, add a short note **only if needed**: placeholders to fill in, inputs that would make it stronger, or an assumption you made.
- If the user asks for options, give 2–3 alternative hooks (just lines 1–2) rather than whole new posts. The hook is what gets tested.

## Quality check before returning

- Would lines 1–2 make the target reader click "see more"?
- Are there two blank lines between the opening and the body?
- Is anything clumped? Titles on their own line, and no paragraph packing in several ideas.
- Is every client, company or person either from the author or a `[placeholder]`, and are the numbers you filled in listed in the note?
- Can someone get the main point by skimming only the hook, the section headers and the `→`, `-` or numbered lines?
- Does it end with a comment-keyword CTA for something real, or a question if there's nothing to offer?
- If there's a comment keyword, does it match the resource, and is it one word in all caps?
- Is it under 3,000 characters, ideally 800–1,300 characters?
- Is it written in the positive, with no stacked "No…" fragments, "not X, but Y" lines or "Never…" commands?
- Does it contain no markdown, no throat-clearing opener and no generic filler ("in today's fast-paced world", "game-changer")?
- Is it useful to a reader who never comments?
- Next to another post from this skill, would it read like the same template with new words? If so, change the shape.
