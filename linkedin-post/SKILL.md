# LinkedIn Post Engine

Turn a topic and the author's own facts into a post that's ready to paste. Every post uses the same mechanics: a hook that stops the scroll, short lines that are easy to skim, specific numbers, and one CTA that asks readers to reply in the comments. The four formats differ in what job the post does and how it is built.

## 1. Gather inputs

| Input | Needed for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Post type | all | Lead magnet, case study, educational, or viral lead magnet. If the user didn't choose, pick one using the table in section 2 and say which. |
| Topic | all | Required. If it's missing, ask. |
| Result / metric | case study, viral lead magnet | e.g. "100k views", "500 leads", "$40k MRR". |
| Timeframe | case study, viral lead magnet | e.g. "60 days". |
| Target audience | all (optional) | Sets the vocabulary, the pain points and which examples to use. |
| Context from the author | all (optional) | Stories, facts, numbers, the resource on offer, tone preferences. This is the most valuable input. |

Rules for inputs:
- **Use the author's context as written and never contradict it.** If they say the campaign took 90 days, don't write 60.
- **Never invent proof.** Don't make up metrics, client names, testimonials, follower counts or "used by 500+ marketers" claims. If a format needs a number the user didn't give, write a bracketed placeholder such as `[X]` or `[# of people who've used it]` and list the placeholders after the post.
- **Lead magnet formats need a real resource.** If the user hasn't said what will be sent (a guide, template, checklist, Notion doc, video), ask, or write `[RESOURCE]` and point it out. A comment CTA with nothing behind it costs the author trust.
- Don't stop to ask about optional fields. Draft with what you have and note what would make the post stronger.

## 2. Pick the format

| Format | Use it when | The post's job |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead magnet** | The author has a free resource to give away | Get comments (reach) and collect leads |
| **Case study** | The author has a real before/after with numbers | Prove results and start sales conversations |
| **Educational** | The author knows things worth teaching but has nothing to give away | Build authority and earn follows and saves |
| **Viral lead magnet** | The author did (or will do) a large analysis of well-known people or brands | Get the most reach possible and grow followers fast |

Choosing between similar formats:
- If the resource is a small checklist or template, use **lead magnet**. If it's a deep teardown with many frameworks, use **viral lead magnet**.
- If the user has results but no resource, use **case study**. If they have expertise but no results to cite, use **educational**.

## 3. Shared rules (all four formats)

These are the "critical formatting rules" from the original prompts, with more detail on each.

**1. Hook: open with a bold, attention-grabbing first line.**
LinkedIn hides everything after roughly the first 2–3 lines (about 200 characters on desktop, less on mobile) behind "…see more". Lines 1–2 must earn the click. "Bold" means bold content, not bold formatting. Patterns that work:
- A specific result: "We went from 0 to 12,000 followers in 90 days."
- A contrarian claim: "Cold email isn't dead. Your first line is."
- Time or effort invested: "I spent 11 hours breaking down 40 of Apple's launch emails."
- A curiosity gap with a number: "7 hiring mistakes that cost me $200k."
- A direct callout to the audience: "Founders: stop writing your own LinkedIn posts like blog intros."

Avoid warm-ups such as "I'm excited to share", "In today's world" and "Let's talk about…", and avoid starting with a question the reader can answer "no" to.

The second line should either add tension ("Most people get this wrong.") or promise a payoff ("Here's exactly how:").

**2. Short sentences, generous line breaks.**
Keep sentences to about 1–2 lines on mobile. Put a blank line between most lines. Paragraphs should almost never be longer than 2 sentences. White space is what makes a post skimmable on LinkedIn.

**3. Visual hierarchy using plain-text symbols.**
- `→` for benefits, results and "this leads to that" lines
- `-` for plain lists
- `1.` `2.` `3.` for steps or ranked points

Use a few emoji at most, and only if the author's voice uses them. Don't put an emoji at the start of every line.

**4. Specifics everywhere.**
Write exact numbers, timeframes and results ("37%", "in 21 days", "from 3 to 41 demos/month"), not "a lot", "quickly" or "significant growth". Specific details are what make a post believable. (They still have to come from the author; see section 1.)

**5. Social proof or credibility markers.**
Examples: who it was used by, results it produced, the author's relevant experience ("After running ads for 40+ SaaS brands…"), or well-known names the work analyzes. Only use proof the author supplied, or placeholders.

**6. A clear comment CTA: "Comment '[WORD]' and I'll send it to you."**
- Choose one short, all-caps keyword that matches the resource: GUIDE, TEMPLATE, PLAYBOOK, SWIPE, BREAKDOWN, CHECKLIST.
- Keep it to one word that's easy to type on a phone and not something people would comment anyway (avoid YES or WOW).
- Comments push the post's distribution, and the keyword shows the author who wants the resource.
- How strongly to push the CTA depends on the format (see section 4).

**7. Scannable.**
A reader who only sees the first two lines plus anything formatted with `→`, `-` or numbers should still get the main point.

**Tone:** conversational and direct, written in the first person. Use "you" more than "I" except in story parts. Cut filler, every line should earn its place. The post must be useful even to someone who never comments.

**Length:** LinkedIn allows 3,000 characters. Aim for:
- Lead magnet: 800–1,300 characters
- Case study: 900–1,500
- Educational: 1,200–2,200
- Viral lead magnet: 1,300–2,200

**Hashtags:** none by default. If the user wants them, add 0–3 relevant ones at the very end.

## 4. Format playbooks

### Lead magnet

The job is to give away a valuable resource in exchange for a comment.

Structure:
1. **Hook**: a bold claim or striking statement related to the problem the resource solves.
2. **Problem**: 2–4 short lines describing the pain in the reader's own words. Make them feel recognized.
3. **Solution preview**: "So I put together [resource]. Inside:" followed by 3–6 `→` or `-` bullets. Each bullet should be concrete and a little tempting ("→ The 3-line cold email that booked 14 calls last month", not "→ Email tips").
4. **Social proof**: one line, e.g. "Used by 500+ marketers", "Built from 3 years of running this at [Company]".
5. **CTA**: "Comment 'GUIDE' and I'll send it to you."
6. **Bonus**: "Repost this and I'll also send you [extra bonus]." Only include this if the author actually has a bonus; otherwise leave it out or use a placeholder.

Advice:
- The bullets do the selling. Each one should promise a specific result or reveal a specific piece of the resource.
- Optional: add "(Connect with me so I can DM you)", since LinkedIn limits DMs to people who aren't connections.

### Case study

The job is to prove a result with a dramatic before/after, then turn readers' interest into conversations.

Structure:
1. **Transformation hook**: "[X] days ago: [before]. Today: [after]." Both sides should be concrete and measurable.
2. **Timeline**: name the exact period (60 days, 90 days, Q3).
3. **Quantified results** as `→` bullets, 3–5 of them: "→ 4,200 → 31,000 monthly visitors", "→ CAC down 38%".
4. **Brief method**: "Here's how we did it:" then 3–5 numbered steps. Each step needs to be specific enough that someone could copy it. Don't write "we focused on quality content".
5. **Key lesson**: "The takeaway?" then one sharp sentence the reader can apply.
6. **Soft CTA**: "Want similar results? Let's talk." or "DM me 'AUDIT'".

Advice:
- This format deliberately uses a softer CTA than shared rule 6, because its goal is to start sales conversations. If the author also has a resource (such as the template used), add a comment-keyword line as well.
- A client's name or permission matters. If the context doesn't name the client, describe them ("a 12-person B2B SaaS team"), never invent a name.
- Credibility comes from honesty. Mentioning one thing that didn't work makes the post more believable.

### Educational

The job is to build authority with insights people can act on.

Structure:
1. **Clear promise**: "NEVER [struggle] again with [topic]." Other options: "[N] [topic] lessons I wish I knew [timeframe] ago" or "How to [result] without [common pain]."
2. **Framework**: 5–7 numbered points.
3. **Each point** = an insight plus how to apply it. Give the point a short title line, then 1–2 lines on how to put it into practice. Format: `1. [Insight]` then `→ [How to apply it / example]`.
4. **Specific examples** in at least half of the points: real numbers, tools or before/after phrasing.
5. **Closing CTA** for deeper engagement, e.g. "Comment 'CHEATSHEET' and I'll send the full version", "Which one are you skipping? Tell me below", or "Save this for your next [situation]."

Advice:
- Put the strongest or most surprising point first or second. Readers drop off quickly.
- Each point should make sense on its own, so it could be screenshotted.
- If the author has no resource, use a question CTA or a save/repost CTA instead of a keyword they can't fulfil.

### Viral lead magnet

The job is to get as much reach as possible by teasing a comprehensive analysis (8–16 hours of work) of well-known names.

Structure:
1. **Hook**: an impressive case study or analysis, e.g. "I analyzed 100 of Hormozi's top posts. 9 patterns showed up again and again."
2. **Tease the effort**: "I spent [8+] hours breaking down…" The work invested is what makes it valuable.
3. **What's inside**: 6–10 `→` bullets listing frameworks, strategies and examples ("→ The 3-part hook formula behind his 5 most-shared posts").
4. **Name-drop recognizable figures or brands** in the hook and the bullets. Familiar names stop people scrolling.
5. **Triple CTA**:
   "Want it?
   1. Like this post
   2. Comment 'BREAKDOWN'
   3. Connect with me (so I can DM you)"
6. **Bonus**: "I'll also send you a 30-min walkthrough video." Only include it if one exists or will exist; otherwise use a placeholder.

Advice:
- Only name-drop people or brands whose public work is actually analyzed. Don't imply they endorse the author or worked with them.
- If the analysis doesn't exist yet, write the post with placeholders and tell the author what they need to produce before posting.
- If the user gave a result or timeframe, use them as credibility, e.g. "These same frameworks got me 100k views in 60 days."

## 5. Output

- **Output only the post text, ready to paste into LinkedIn.** Plain text, no markdown (no `**bold**`, no headings), and no preamble like "Here's your post:". Unicode such as `→` is fine.
- After the post, separated by a line, add a short note **only if needed**: placeholders to fill in, inputs that would make it stronger, or an assumption you made (such as which format you chose).
- If the user asks for options, give 2–3 alternative hooks (just lines 1–2) rather than whole new posts. The hook is what gets tested.

## 6. Quality check before returning

- Would lines 1–2 make the target reader click "see more"?
- Is every number either from the author or a visible `[placeholder]`?
- Can someone get the main point by skimming only the `→`, `-` and numbered lines?
- Is there exactly one main CTA, and does it match the format (comment keyword, soft "let's talk", or the triple CTA)?
- Does the comment keyword match the resource, and is it one word in all caps?
- Is it under 3,000 characters and inside the target range for the format?
- Does it contain no markdown, no throat-clearing opener and no generic filler ("in today's fast-paced world", "game-changer")?
- Is it useful to a reader who never comments?
